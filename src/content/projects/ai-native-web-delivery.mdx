I run web channel delivery for a multinational hotel loyalty program. Three
pods, 72 people across product, engineering, QA and design, twelve launches so
far. Velocity is up about 1.5× on where it started.

The gain came from putting the tooling inside the workflow instead of offering
it alongside. That distinction is most of what I have learned here, and it took
me longer to see than it should have.

## The constraint

Nothing paused. Twelve releases went out while the way of working changed
underneath them, which ruled out most of what a transformation program would
normally do. No pilot cohort, no parallel track, no month of enablement before
the real work resumed.

## What I owned

Delivery across the three pods. Unblocking, clarifying requirements and
designs, and holding funding through quarterly SteerCo. Tooling adoption inside
the pods sat with me too.

## Getting the tools used

Claude, GitHub Copilot, Playwright and Rovo. Which tools we picked mattered
less than whether using them was optional, and for the first couple of months
they were optional.

That version did not work. Two or three engineers who would have found the
tools anyway used them well, everyone else carried on, and I had some nice
anecdotes and no movement in the numbers. What changed it was making the
default path run through the tooling. Generated tests became how QA works
rather than something QA could try if a sprint went well.

Where each one landed:

- **Claude** for requirements clarification and spec drafting
- **GitHub Copilot** for in-editor throughput
- **Playwright** for test automation, which is what gates a release date
- **Rovo** for retrieval across the Atlassian sprawl a program this size grows

The 1.5× showed up in build throughput and in feature fix time, meaning how
long it takes to correct a defect and prove it corrected.

Both are cycle-time problems. This program never lacked ideas about what to
build. It lost time in the gap between finding something broken and confirming
it was fixed, and compressing that gap moved the release calendar. Better specs
would have queued behind the same bottleneck.

It is also why embedding beat choosing. Fix time gets set by whatever the
slowest pod does under pressure.

> **Draft note —** Two things would sharpen this. Roughly what was fix time
> before and after? And did all three pods move at the same rate? A pod that
> lagged is the more interesting story, because it says what embedding costs.

## The launches

Twelve, through a consistent three-phase approach. Global promotions, partner
and employee rate validations, AI chatbots, next-generation flyout menus.

The phases existed because programs this size fail at handoffs rather than at
execution. Every pod knowing what "ready" meant at each boundary was worth more
than any individual process improvement, and it gave SteerCo a shape they
recognised when it came time to fund the next quarter.

## Outcomes

- **12 launches** across promotions, rate validation, conversational AI and
  navigation
- **1.5× launch velocity** after the tooling went into pod workflows
- **+4% account enrollment**
- Funding and alignment held across quarterly SteerCo cycles

## What I'd do differently

A few people turned into informal tooling coaches inside their pods. It worked
and it was fragile. The role had no name, no time allocation and no
recognition, and if any one of them had rolled off I think the whole thing
would have slid back. I should have named it early.

I also rebuilt the velocity baseline after the fact. The 1.5× holds up, but I
would rather have had clean pre-period numbers than a good reconstruction, and
it did not occur to me to capture them before we started changing things.
