I run web channel delivery for a multinational hotel loyalty program. Three
development pods, 72 people across product, engineering, QA and design, 12
launches so far. Launch velocity is 1.5× where it started.

The velocity came from putting AI tooling inside the workflow instead of
offering it alongside. That distinction is most of what I have learned on this
engagement.

## The situation

The launch calendar did not slow down for a methodology change. Twelve releases
went out while the way of working changed underneath them. Anything that
required pausing delivery was not an option, which ruled out most of what a
transformation program would normally do.

## What I owned

Delivery across all three pods. Unblocking teams, clarifying requirements and
designs, and securing funding through quarterly SteerCo planning. AI tooling
adoption inside the pods sat with me as well.

## Embedding AI in the workflow

The tools were Claude, GitHub Copilot, Playwright and Rovo. Which tools mattered
less than whether using them was optional.

Optional tooling gets picked up by the two or three people who would have found
it anyway. You get anecdotes. Embedded tooling means the default path through
the work runs through the tool: generated tests are how QA works now, not
something QA could try if it has time.

Where each landed:

- **Claude** for requirements clarification and spec drafting
- **GitHub Copilot** for in-editor throughput
- **Playwright** for test automation, which is what gates a release date
- **Rovo** for context retrieval across the Atlassian sprawl a program this
  size accumulates

The 1.5× showed up in build throughput and in feature fix time. Fix time is how
long it takes to correct a defect and prove it fixed.

Both are cycle-time problems. This program never lacked ideas about what to
build. It lost time in the loop between finding something wrong and confirming
it was right again. Compress that loop and the release calendar moves. Leave it
and better specs queue behind the same bottleneck.

That also explains why embedding beat choosing. Fix time is set by what the
slowest pod does under pressure, not by what the most enthusiastic engineer
adopts.

## The launches

Twelve, run through a consistent three-phase approach: global promotions,
partner and employee rate validations, AI chatbots, and next-generation flyout
menus.

The phase structure existed because programs this size fail at handoffs rather
than at execution. Consistent phases meant every pod knew what "ready" meant at
each boundary, and SteerCo could fund against a shape it recognised.

## Outcomes

- **12 launches** across promotions, rate validation, conversational AI and
  navigation
- **1.5× launch velocity** after embedding AI tooling in pod workflows
- **+4% account enrollment**
- Funding and alignment held across quarterly SteerCo cycles

## What I'd do differently

A few people became informal tooling coaches inside their pods. That worked,
and it was fragile. The role was unnamed, unfunded and unrecognised, and it
would have collapsed if any of them had rolled off. I should have named it and
given it explicit time.

I also reconstructed the velocity baseline after the fact. The 1.5× holds up,
but I would rather have had clean pre-period instrumentation than a good
reconstruction, and I did not think to set it up before we started changing
things.
