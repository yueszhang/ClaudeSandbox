I'm the program and product lead for the web channels of a major multinational
hotel loyalty program. Multiple development pods spanning product, engineering,
QA and design, running against a multi-launch calendar that includes two global
promotions.

The work I'd point to is sign-in. We migrated a modal login to a flyout
pattern, phased across five releases at roughly 180 to 220 story points. Login
is the least glamorous surface on a loyalty site and the one every other
journey depends on, so it gets deferred until it can't be.

## The constraint

The launch calendar didn't slow down for a methodology change. Releases went out
while the way of working changed underneath them, which ruled out most of what
a transformation program would normally do. No pilot cohort, no parallel track,
no month of enablement before real work resumed.

## What I owned

Delivery across the pods. Unblocking teams, clarifying requirements and designs,
and holding cross-functional alignment through quarterly planning. AI tooling
adoption inside the pods sat with me too.

## The two-day deprecation

The story I'd tell in an interview is smaller than the sign-in work and says
more about the job.

Two partner brands had to come off the site, urgently. That turned out to be 45
references across 8 pages, touching three cross-discipline teams. We turned it
around in two days.

Nothing about that is technically hard. It's hard because 45 references is the
kind of number nobody knows until someone goes and counts, and because three
teams on a two-day clock will each assume another one owns the ambiguous cases.
Most of the work was finding all 45 and deciding who had which.

## Embedding AI in the workflow

The tools were Claude, GitHub Copilot, Playwright and Rovo. Which tools mattered
less than whether using them was optional.

Optional tooling gets picked up by the two or three people who would have found
it anyway. You get anecdotes. Embedded tooling means the default path through
the work runs through the tool: generated tests are how QA works now, not
something QA could try if it has time.

Where each landed:

- **Claude** for requirements clarification and spec drafting
- **GitHub Copilot** for in-editor throughput
- **Playwright** for test automation, which is what gates a release date
- **Rovo** for context retrieval across the Atlassian sprawl a program this
  size accumulates

Where it helped most was cycle time rather than throughput of ideas. This
program never lacked things to build. It lost time in the loop between finding
something wrong and confirming it was right again, and that loop is set by
whatever the slowest pod does under pressure rather than by what the most
enthusiastic engineer adopts. Which is the argument for embedding over
offering.

## The launch calendar

Global promotions, partner and employee rate validations, and the flyout
navigation work, run through a consistent phase structure.

That structure existed because programs this size fail at handoffs rather than
at execution. Every pod knowing what "ready" meant at each boundary was worth
more than any individual process improvement.

The other durable piece was partner-agnostic enrollment and cross-channel
authentication between the apps and the web. Both are the kind of plumbing that
only gets noticed when it's missing.

- Sign-in centralized from a modal to a flyout pattern, phased across **5

  web

- Two global promotions delivered inside the launch calendar

## What I'd do differently

A few people became informal tooling coaches inside their pods. That worked, and
it was fragile. The role was unnamed, unfunded and unrecognized, and it would
have collapsed if any of them had rolled off. I should have named it early.

The bigger miss was instrumentation. I didn't set up a clean before-and-after
baseline before we started changing how the pods worked, which is why the
velocity claim on my resume is harder to source than it should be. On a change
you intend to claim credit for, the measurement has to exist before the change
does.
