This is the engagement I point to when someone asks whether "AI-native" means
anything operationally. Same pods, same client, same delivery pressure — and
launch velocity moved 1.5× because the tooling went *into* the workflow rather
than being offered next to it.

## The situation

Web channels for a multinational hotel loyalty program. Three development pods
totaling 72 people across product, engineering, QA, and design, with a launch
calendar that did not slow down for methodology experiments.

The constraint that shapes everything here: you cannot pause enterprise
delivery to transform it. Whatever changed had to change while twelve launches
went out the door.

## What I owned

Delivery across all three pods — unblocking teams, clarifying requirements and
designs, and securing funding and cross-functional alignment through quarterly
SteerCo planning. Plus the AI tooling adoption inside the pods.

## Embedding AI in the workflow

The tools were Claude, GitHub Copilot, Playwright, and Rovo. The decision that
mattered was not which tools — it was refusing to treat them as an optional
productivity layer.

Optional tooling gets used by the three people who were going to seek it out
anyway. That produces anecdotes, not velocity. Embedded tooling means the
default path through the work runs through the tool: test generation is part of
how QA works, not a thing QA could try.

Where each landed:

- **Claude** — requirements clarification, spec drafting, and the analysis work
  that used to consume the front half of every cycle
- **GitHub Copilot** — in-editor engineering throughput
- **Playwright** — test automation, the highest-leverage single change, because
  regression coverage is what actually gates a release date
- **Rovo** — search and context retrieval across the sprawl of Atlassian
  artifacts that accumulate on a program this size

The 1.5× came disproportionately from compressing the parts of delivery that
were never writing code: clarifying what was being built, and proving it still
worked.

## The launches

Twelve, run through a consistent three-phase approach: global promotions,
partner and employee rate validations, AI chatbots, and next-generation flyout
menus.

The three-phase structure existed because a program with 72 people and multiple
stakeholder groups fails at handoffs, not at execution. Consistent phases meant
every pod knew what "ready" meant at each boundary, and SteerCo could fund
against a shape it recognized.

## Outcomes

- **12 launches** delivered across promotions, rate validation, conversational
  AI, and navigation
- **1.5× launch velocity** after embedding AI tooling in pod workflows
- **+4% account enrollment**
- Funding and alignment secured across quarterly SteerCo cycles

## What I'd do differently

I underestimated how much of the velocity gain depended on a few people who
became informal tooling coaches inside their pods. That worked, but it was
fragile and unrecognized. I would name the role and give it explicit time
rather than letting it run on goodwill.

I would also have baselined more carefully before starting. The 1.5× is real
and defensible, but I reconstructed the baseline partly after the fact, and I
would rather have had clean pre-period instrumentation than a good
reconstruction.
