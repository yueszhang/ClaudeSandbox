I lead AI adoption across 900+ product teams at a global investment manager.
Feature delivery has doubled since we activated the change plan.

The target was autonomous maturity. Teams picking AI-assisted approaches on
their own, on work that matters, without a central team asking them to. Two
things had to move together to get there. Teams needed sanctioned access to
models and tooling. They also needed a reason to change how they worked while
already under delivery pressure.

Solve only the first and you buy an expensive platform nobody opens. Solve only
the second and adoption routes around governance, which in a regulated firm is
worse than no adoption.

## The situation

900+ product teams across enough business lines that no central function could
hand-hold them. The firm is regulated, so "try things and see what happens" was
never on the table.

I came in after the pilot phase. Several teams had done promising work with AI
tooling. None of it had reached anyone else.

## What I owned

AI strategy and enablement for the full 900+ team population. In practice that
was three workstreams:

- The maturity model and its stage gates
- Evaluating and sequencing the enablement stack
- The change program that connected the two

## The maturity model

We defined four stages with an explicit gate between each one.

<Diagram name="maturity" />

The gates did the work. A team's stage had to be observable from outside rather
than self-reported. Self-assessment is how programs report green while nothing
changes on the ground.

Gates also gave teams a concrete next action. "Adopt AI" is not a task. "Get
your repository to the point where an agent can work in it" is.

## The enablement stack

I identified and vetted the infrastructure that had to exist before adoption
was possible:

- **MCP, agent and skills marketplace** — capability gets shared rather than
  rebuilt by each team
- **Bedrock environments** for sanctioned, governed model access
- **Agent-ready codebases**, structured so agents can work in them productively
- **One-click installers**
- **LLM/SLM gateways** for routing, cost control and usage visibility
- **Responsible AI training**, non-negotiable in a regulated firm

The one-click installer mattered more than the model choice. Setup friction was
where teams stalled, and a team that loses an afternoon to environment
configuration does not come back next sprint.

## The change plan

Three moves, running in parallel.

**Champions.** We found practitioners already doing the work and gave them
visibility and air cover. Teams believe the pod next to them. They do not
believe a central function.

**Internal thought leadership.** Specific to this firm's stack and constraints.
Generic AI content does not survive contact with a regulated enterprise,
because the first question is always "can we even do that here."

**Executive workshops.** 30+ sessions with executive leaders and product
groups. These were working sessions, not presentations. Product group leaders
left owning part of the plan. A change program that lives only in the central
team is a document.

## Outcomes

- Adoption moving toward autonomous maturity across **900+ product teams**
- **Feature delivery doubled**: twice the launches in a comparable window, with
  comparable resources. Not more people and not a longer runway
- **30+ workshops** with executive leaders and product groups
- Enablement stack vetted and sequenced for enterprise rollout

## What I'd do differently

I should have instrumented adoption on day one. We had good signal on teams
engaged with the program and almost none on the rest. At 900 teams, the teams
you are not hearing from are the program. Usage telemetry from the gateway
should have been a launch requirement rather than something we wanted once the
stack existed.

I also spent too long on evaluation breadth. Teams did not want six options.
They wanted one default with a good on-ramp, and the months spent comparing
tools would have been better spent making the first choice easy to start with.
