I lead AI enablement for a global investment manager. Around 900 product teams
sit in scope. Feature delivery has roughly doubled since we switched the change
plan on.

The goal we set was that teams would reach for AI tooling without anyone
telling them to. That turned out to be two problems wearing one hat. Teams
needed access they were allowed to use, which is infrastructure. They also
needed a reason to change how they worked mid-sprint, which isn't.

We spent the first stretch mostly on the infrastructure side. My read now is
that the sequencing was about right and I badly underestimated how little
access moves on its own.

## Where things stood

I came in after the pilots. Several teams had done good work with AI tooling
and none of it had travelled. That is the normal state of an enterprise AI
program and it is worth being blunt about it, because the pilot is the part
everyone can already do.

The firm is regulated. "Try things and see what happens" was never available,
which shaped more of the design than any technology choice did.

## What I owned

AI strategy and enablement for the full 900-team population. Three workstreams:
the maturity model and its gates, the enablement stack, and the change program
that connected them.

## The maturity model

Four stages, with a gate between each one. The stages themselves were not the
clever part and I would guess most enablement programs land somewhere similar.

<Diagram name="maturity" />

The gates were the part that worked. A team's stage had to be visible from
outside rather than claimed, because self-assessment is how these programs end
up reporting green while nothing moves.

They also gave teams something to do. "Adopt AI" is not a task. "Get your repo
into a state where an agent can work in it" is a task, and a team can finish it
on a Thursday.

> **Draft note —** What did each gate require in practice? A checklist, a
> review, an automated signal off the gateway? This is the most reusable thing
> here and the page currently describes it in the abstract. One worked example
> of a team clearing a gate would be worth more than the model diagram.

## The enablement stack

I vetted the infrastructure that had to exist before any of this was possible:

- **MCP, agent and skills marketplace**, so capability gets shared instead of
  rebuilt team by team
- **Bedrock environments** for sanctioned model access
- **Agent-ready codebases**
- **One-click installers**
- **LLM/SLM gateways** for routing, cost control and usage visibility
- **Responsible AI training**, which in a regulated firm is not optional

The installer mattered more than the model choice, which surprised me at the
time. Setup friction was where teams stalled. A team that burns an afternoon on
environment configuration does not come back to it next sprint, and I watched
that happen enough times to stop treating it as a minor item.

> **Draft note —** Which of these shipped, and in what order? Sequencing is
> where the judgement shows, and a list flattens it. If some are still pending,
> say so. An honest roadmap reads better than implied completeness.

## The change plan

Three things running at once, and none of them worked in isolation.

We found practitioners already doing the work and gave them visibility and some
cover. Teams believe the pod next to them. They do not believe a central
function, and I say that as the central function.

We wrote internal material specific to this firm's stack and constraints.
Generic AI content dies on contact with a regulated enterprise, because the
first question in the room is always whether any of it is allowed here.

And I ran 30-odd workshops with executive leaders and product groups. Working
sessions rather than presentations, with the aim that product group leaders
left owning a piece of the plan. A change program that lives only in the
central team is a document.

## Outcomes

- Adoption moving toward autonomous maturity across **900+ product teams**
- **Feature delivery doubled**: twice the launches in a comparable window, on
  comparable resources
- **30+ workshops** with executive leaders and product groups
- Enablement stack vetted and sequenced for rollout

## What I'd do differently

Instrumentation should have been a day-one requirement. We had good signal on
the teams engaged with the program and close to none on everyone else, which at
900 teams is the majority of the thing I was supposedly managing. Usage
telemetry off the gateway would have cost very little to set up at the start
and we did not get it until the stack was already live.

I also spent too long comparing tools. Teams wanted one default with a decent
on-ramp. The comparison work was thorough and I think most of it was wasted.
