Most enterprise AI programs die in the same place. A pilot goes well, a
leadership deck gets made, and then nothing reaches the teams who would have to
change how they work. The pilot was never the hard part.

This engagement started from the other end: assume the destination is
*autonomous maturity* — teams choosing AI-assisted approaches without being
asked, on work that matters — and then work backwards to what has to be true
for 900+ product teams to get there.

## The situation

A global investment management firm with 900+ product teams. Enough scale that
no central team could hand-hold adoption, and enough regulatory weight that
"just try things" was not an available strategy.

Two problems had to be solved at once, and solving either alone would have
failed:

- **Infrastructure readiness.** Teams cannot adopt what they cannot access.
  Without a sanctioned path to models, tools, and agent-ready code, adoption
  routes around governance or doesn't happen.
- **Behavior.** Access does not produce usage. Teams under delivery pressure do
  not adopt new methods because a strategy document told them to.

## What I owned

AI strategy and enablement across the full 900+ team population — the adoption
model, the enablement stack evaluation, and the change program that connected
them.

## The maturity model

Adoption isn't binary, and treating it that way is why programs report green
while nothing changes. We defined staged maturity with explicit gates between
stages, so a team's level was an observable fact rather than a self-assessment.

Stage gates did two things. They gave teams a concrete next step instead of an
abstract mandate, and they gave leadership a real read on distribution —
how many teams sat at each stage, and which ones were stuck.

## The enablement stack

I identified and vetted the infrastructure that had to exist before adoption
was even possible:

- **An MCP, agent, and skills marketplace** — so capability is shared and
  discoverable rather than rebuilt per team
- **Bedrock environments** — sanctioned, governed model access
- **Agent-ready codebases** — repositories structured so agents can work in
  them productively
- **One-click installers** — because setup friction is where adoption actually
  dies
- **LLM/SLM gateways** — routing, cost control, and observability
- **Responsible AI training** — the non-negotiable in a regulated industry

The unifying principle: every item removes a reason a motivated team would
otherwise fail to adopt. Enablement is mostly the removal of excuses, and the
one-click installer matters more than it sounds like it should.

## The change plan

Infrastructure without change management produces expensive unused platforms.
The plan ran on three moves:

**Identify champions.** Existing practitioners already doing the work, made
visible and given air cover. Peer proof outperforms mandates — a team believes
the pod next to them far more than it believes a central function.

**Build thought leadership.** Internal, concrete, and specific to this firm's
stack and constraints. Generic AI content does not survive contact with a
regulated enterprise.

**Align executives in workshops.** 30+ sessions with executive leaders and
product groups. These were not presentations. Product group leaders had to
leave owning a piece of the plan, because a change program that only lives in
the central team is not a change program.

## Outcomes

- Adoption driven toward autonomous maturity across **900+ product teams**
- **Doubled feature delivery** following change plan activation
- **30+ workshops** aligning executive leaders and product groups
- An enablement stack vetted and sequenced for enterprise-wide rollout

## What I'd do differently

I would instrument adoption earlier. We had strong signal on the teams engaged
with the program and much weaker signal on the silent majority — and at 900
teams, the silent majority *is* the program. Usage telemetry from the gateway
should have been a day-one requirement rather than something we wanted once the
stack was live.

I would also spend less time on tool evaluation breadth and more on depth in
the top two or three. Teams did not need six options. They needed one obvious
default with a good on-ramp.
