I lead AI strategy and enablement for a firm-wide adoption program at a leading
investment management company. **783 product teams across seven divisions** sit
in scope, moving up a four-level maturity curve.

The destination is Autonomized: agents owning defined stages of delivery with
humans on the approval gates. The nearer target, and the one that matters
commercially, is Augmented. Two things had to move together to get anywhere.
Teams needed sanctioned access to models and tooling. They also needed a reason
to change how they worked while already under delivery pressure.

Solve only the first and you buy an expensive platform nobody opens. Solve only
the second and adoption routes around governance, which in a regulated firm is
worse than no adoption.

## The situation

Seven divisions, 783 teams, and no realistic way for a central function to
hand-hold any of them. The firm is regulated, so "try things and
see what happens" was never on the table.

I came in after the pilot phase. Several teams had done promising work with AI
tooling. None of it had reached anyone else.

## What I owned

AI strategy and enablement across the full team population. The work ran as
four workstreams over six months:

- **Standards.** The maturity model, its levels, and the gates between them
- **Capabilities.** Evaluating and sequencing the enablement stack
- **Change management.** Champions, curricula, internal thought leadership
- **Measurement.** How a team's level gets assessed rather than claimed

## The maturity model

Four levels, with an explicit gate between each one: Activating, Augmented,
Accelerated, Autonomized.

<Diagram name="maturity" />

Each level is assessed against six characteristics rather than a single score.
AI-powered delivery practices, agent-ready codebase, autonomous agent
utilization, team autonomy and enablement, responsible AI, and AI-augmented
operations. A team can be strong on tooling and weak on responsible AI, and the
model is meant to show that rather than average it away.

Augmented is the level that matters commercially. It's where a team is applying
AI to real delivery work rather than experimenting alongside it, and it's the
2026 target.

The gates did the work. A team's stage had to be observable from outside rather
than self-reported. Self-assessment is how programs report green while nothing
changes on the ground.

Gates also gave teams a concrete next action. "Adopt AI" isn't a task. "Get
your repository to the point where an agent can work in it" is.

## The enablement stack

I identified and vetted the infrastructure that had to exist before adoption
was possible:

- **MCP, agent and skills marketplace** — capability gets shared rather than
  rebuilt by each team
- **Bedrock environments** for sanctioned, governed model access
- **Agent-ready codebases**, structured so agents can work in them productively
- **One-click installers**
- **LLM/SLM gateways** for routing, cost control and usage visibility
- **Responsible AI training**, non-negotiable in a regulated firm

The one-click installer mattered more than the model choice. Setup friction was
where teams stalled, and a team that loses an afternoon to environment
configuration doesn't come back next sprint.

## The change plan

Three moves, running in parallel.

**Champions.** We found practitioners already doing the work and gave them
visibility and air cover. Teams believe the pod next to them. They don't
believe a central function.

**Internal thought leadership.** Specific to this firm's stack and constraints.
Generic AI content doesn't survive contact with a regulated enterprise,
because the first question is always "can we even do that here."

**Executive workshops.** Working sessions with executive leaders and product
groups rather than presentations, with the aim that product group leaders left
owning a piece of the plan. A change program that lives only in the central
team is a document.

Underneath all three sat a recognition architecture, running from individual
badges up to team certification, built on the client's existing infrastructure
rather than a new system nobody would log into.

## What I'd do differently

I should have instrumented adoption on day one. We had good signal on teams
engaged with the program and almost none on the rest. At this scale, the teams
you aren't hearing from are the program. Usage telemetry from the gateway
should have been a launch requirement rather than something we wanted once the
stack existed.

I also spent too long on evaluation breadth. Teams didn't want six options.
They wanted one default with a good on-ramp, and the months spent comparing
tools would have been better spent making the first choice easy to start with.
