Replatforming an award-winning program is a specific kind of hard. Nothing is
visibly broken, everyone can name what they'd lose, and the case for change has
to be made against a thing that is currently winning awards.

## The situation

A global travel loyalty program on infrastructure it had outgrown. The
replatform target was a SaaS-based provider — which meant the real question was
not *whether* to move but what could not be compromised in the move.

## What I owned

The product roadmap: prioritizing 20+ next-generation features including AI and
gamification capabilities, and defining 400+ core and future-state requirements
through 25+ discovery sessions with C-suite executives, designers, developers,
and end-users. Then leading customization and pricing negotiations across five
loyalty vendors.

## Requirements as leverage

Four hundred requirements sounds like documentation overhead. It was the
negotiating position.

Vendor negotiations go badly when the buyer's requirements are vague, because
vague requirements get filled in by the vendor's roadmap and priced as
customization. Every requirement we could state precisely — sourced from an
actual discovery session with an actual stakeholder — was a thing that could be
scoped, compared across five vendors, and priced before anyone was committed.

The split between *core* and *future-state* did the heavy lifting. Core
requirements were the floor: fail these and the vendor is disqualified.
Future-state requirements were the roadmap the platform had to not foreclose —
including the AI and gamification features that were still ahead of us.

That distinction is what let us negotiate hard on core capability without
paying premium prices for future-state features we would rather build ourselves
later.

## Discovery across misaligned incentives

Twenty-five discovery sessions across C-suite, design, engineering, and end
users. These groups did not want the same things, and pretending otherwise
produces requirements that satisfy nobody.

Running them separately and reconciling afterward surfaced the genuine
conflicts as conflicts — decisions for leadership to make explicitly, rather
than ambiguities that would resurface mid-build as change requests.

## Outcomes

- **$1.2M in cost savings** through customization and pricing negotiations
  across 5 vendors
- **Successful go-live** on the SaaS platform
- **400+ requirements** defined across core and future state
- **20+ next-generation features** prioritized on the roadmap

## What I'd do differently

I would have cut the requirement count. Four hundred is defensible for a program
this size, but not all four hundred earned their weight, and the long tail cost
real review time from senior stakeholders whose attention was the scarcest
resource on the program. A tighter core with an explicit "we will decide this
during build" bucket would have moved faster without losing much.

## What the future state actually contained

The future-state bucket was not abstract. The gamification track alone covered
points awards, sweepstakes, competition between friends, and badging — four
mechanics that look similar on a slide and are not similar at all underneath.

Points awards are an accounting problem: liability, expiry, and reconciliation.
Sweepstakes are a compliance problem, with rules that differ by jurisdiction.
Competing with friends is a social graph — the only one of the four that needs
a concept of a relationship between members, which most loyalty platforms do
not have. Badging is the cheapest to build and the easiest to get wrong,
because a badge nobody wants is worse than no badge.

Naming them mattered during selection. "Supports gamification" is something
every vendor says. Asking whether the platform models a member-to-member
relationship, or how it handles points liability across jurisdictions, produces
answers that actually differentiate — and it surfaces the customization costs
before you sign rather than after.
