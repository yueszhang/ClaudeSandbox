I owned the roadmap and requirements for moving a global travel loyalty program
onto a SaaS platform. Five vendors in the running, 400-odd requirements
written, $1.2M negotiated out, and a go-live that held.

The money came from the requirements work rather than the negotiation. By the
time we were arguing about price we could describe what we needed more
precisely than any of the vendors could describe what they sold.

## Where things stood

The program had outgrown its infrastructure and was also winning awards, which
is a harder starting position than a broken system. Nobody was complaining.
Everyone could name something they stood to lose.

## What I owned

The roadmap, including 20+ next-generation features across AI and gamification.
I wrote 400+ core and future-state requirements out of 25 discovery sessions
with executives, designers, developers and end users, then ran customization
and pricing negotiations with five vendors.

## Why 400 requirements

It sounds like documentation overhead. In practice it was the whole
negotiating position.

Vague requirements get filled in by the vendor's roadmap and then priced as
customization. Anything we could state precisely was something we could scope,
compare across five bidders, and price before committing to anyone.

Splitting core from future-state did most of the work. Core was the floor: miss
one and the vendor was out. Future-state was the set of things the platform had
to leave open rather than ship on day one, which let us push hard on the floor
without paying a premium for features we would rather build ourselves later.

## What was in the future-state bucket

The gamification track covered points awards, sweepstakes, competing with
friends, and badging. Four mechanics that look interchangeable on a slide and
are not.

Points awards are an accounting problem, with liability and expiry and
reconciliation attached. Sweepstakes are a compliance problem and the rules
move by jurisdiction. Competing with friends needs a social graph, and it is
the only one of the four that requires the platform to model a relationship
between two members, which most loyalty platforms do not do at all. Badging is
the cheapest to build and the easiest to get wrong.

Naming them changed the vendor conversations. Every vendor says they support
gamification. Asking whether the data model holds a member-to-member
relationship gets you an answer that separates them, and it surfaces the
customization cost before signature.

## Discovery with people who wanted different things

25 sessions across C-suite, design, engineering and end users. These groups did
not agree.

I ran them separately and reconciled afterwards, which surfaced the real
conflicts as decisions for leadership to make. Run them together and the
disagreements turn into vague requirements instead, and then they come back
mid-build as change requests.

## Outcomes

- **$1.2M in savings** across customization and pricing with 5 vendors
- Successful go-live on the SaaS platform
- **400+ requirements** across core and future state
- **20+ next-generation features** prioritized

## What I'd do differently

400 was too many. It is defensible for a program this size, and the long tail
still ate review time from senior stakeholders whose attention was the
scarcest thing on the project. A tighter core with an explicit "decide during
build" bucket would have cost us almost nothing and moved faster.
