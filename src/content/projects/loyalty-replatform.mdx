I was product manager on a multi-year transformation replatforming a global
travel loyalty program onto a SaaS provider. I ran discovery, wrote the
requirements, and drove the vendor selection down to five.

The leverage came from the requirements work rather than the negotiation. By
the time we were talking price we could describe what we needed more precisely
than the vendors could describe what they sold.

The program was framed against a prior multi-brand loyalty consolidation, which
meant the organization had done a version of this before and had opinions about
what went wrong last time. That's a better starting position than a blank page,
and a harder one to argue with.

## The situation

The program had outgrown its infrastructure. It had also won awards, which made
the case for change harder than a broken system would have. Nobody was
complaining. Everyone could name something they would lose.

## What I owned

Discovery across three workstreams — loyalty lifecycle management, financial
settlement, and partner and agency integration. The requirements that came out
of it. A capability map and a phased multi-year roadmap. And the RFP itself,
scoped across member lifecycle, tier and points engines, partner integrations,
promotions and settlement.

## Requirements as leverage

Five hundred requirements sounds like documentation overhead. It was the
negotiating position.

Vague requirements get filled in by the vendor's roadmap. Then they get priced
as customization. Every requirement we could state precisely was something we
could scope, compare across five vendors, and price before committing to
anyone.

<Diagram name="requirements" />

Splitting core from future-state did most of the work. Core requirements were
the floor: miss one and the vendor was out. Future-state requirements were
things the platform had to leave open rather than deliver on day one.

That split let us push hard on core capability without paying a premium for
future-state features we would rather build ourselves later.

## What the future state contained

The gamification track covered points awards, sweepstakes, competition between
friends, and badging. Four mechanics that look alike on a slide and aren't
alike underneath.

Points awards are an accounting problem. Liability, expiry, reconciliation.
Sweepstakes are a compliance problem with rules that vary by jurisdiction.
Competing with friends needs a social graph, which is the only one of the four
that requires the platform to model a relationship between two members. Most
loyalty platforms don't. Badging is cheapest to build and easiest to get
wrong, because a badge nobody wants costs you credibility.

Naming them changed the vendor conversation. Every vendor says they support
gamification. Asking whether the data model has a member-to-member relationship
produces an answer that differentiates, and it surfaces customization cost
before signature rather than after.

## Discovery across misaligned incentives

Discovery ran with C-suite, design, engineering and end users. These groups
wanted different things.

I ran them separately and reconciled afterwards. That surfaced genuine
conflicts as conflicts, so leadership could decide them explicitly. Run them
together and the conflicts become ambiguities in the requirements, which
reappear mid-build as change requests.

## What came out of it

- **5 vendors** down-selected and evaluated against a common bar
- **500+ business requirements** across core and future state
- A capability map and a phased multi-year roadmap
- An RFP covering member lifecycle, tier and points engines, partner
  integrations, promotions and settlement

## What I'd do differently

Five hundred was too many. It's defensible for a program this size, and the
long tail still consumed review time from senior stakeholders whose attention
was the scarcest thing on the project. A tighter core with an explicit "decide
during build" bucket would have cost us almost nothing and moved faster.
