I owned the product roadmap and requirements for moving a global travel loyalty
program onto a SaaS platform. Five vendors, 400+ requirements, $1.2M in
negotiated savings, and a go-live that held.

The savings came from the requirements work, not from the negotiation itself.
By the time we were talking price, we could describe what we needed more
precisely than the vendors could describe what they sold.

## The situation

The program had outgrown its infrastructure. It had also won awards, which made
the case for change harder than a broken system would have. Nobody was
complaining. Everyone could name something they would lose.

## What I owned

The product roadmap, including 20+ next-generation features covering AI and
gamification. I defined 400+ core and future-state requirements across 25+
discovery sessions with C-suite executives, designers, developers and end
users. I then led customization and pricing negotiations with five loyalty
vendors.

## Requirements as leverage

400 requirements sounds like documentation overhead. It was the negotiating
position.

Vague requirements get filled in by the vendor's roadmap. Then they get priced
as customization. Every requirement we could state precisely was something we
could scope, compare across five vendors, and price before committing to
anyone.

<Diagram name="requirements" />

Splitting core from future-state did most of the work. Core requirements were
the floor: miss one and the vendor was out. Future-state requirements were
things the platform had to leave open rather than deliver on day one.

That split let us push hard on core capability without paying a premium for
future-state features we would rather build ourselves later.

## What the future state contained

The gamification track covered points awards, sweepstakes, competition between
friends, and badging. Four mechanics that look alike on a slide and are not
alike underneath.

Points awards are an accounting problem. Liability, expiry, reconciliation.
Sweepstakes are a compliance problem with rules that vary by jurisdiction.
Competing with friends needs a social graph, which is the only one of the four
that requires the platform to model a relationship between two members. Most
loyalty platforms do not. Badging is cheapest to build and easiest to get
wrong, because a badge nobody wants costs you credibility.

Naming them changed the vendor conversation. Every vendor says they support
gamification. Asking whether the data model has a member-to-member relationship
produces an answer that differentiates, and it surfaces customization cost
before signature rather than after.

## Discovery across misaligned incentives

25 discovery sessions with C-suite, design, engineering and end users. These
groups wanted different things.

I ran them separately and reconciled afterwards. That surfaced genuine
conflicts as conflicts, so leadership could decide them explicitly. Run them
together and the conflicts become ambiguities in the requirements, which
reappear mid-build as change requests.

## Outcomes

- **$1.2M in savings** across customization and pricing negotiation with 5
  vendors
- Successful go-live on the SaaS platform
- **400+ requirements** across core and future state
- **20+ next-generation features** prioritized

## What I'd do differently

400 was too many. It is defensible for a program this size, but the long tail
consumed review time from senior stakeholders, and their attention was the
scarcest resource on the project. A tighter core with an explicit "decide
during build" bucket would have moved faster and cost us very little.
