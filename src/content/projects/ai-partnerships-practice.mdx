I led an evaluation of six AI tools for Deloitte's Customer Strategy & Design
practice, across synthetic personas, journey mapping and user research. Two
builds were shortlisted, avoiding roughly $400K of spend.

This was internal work, which made it more honest than most vendor evaluations.
Nobody was being sold to. The output either got used by our own practitioners
or it didn't.

## What I owned

The evaluation program. Eight junior practitioners across three pods, one pod
per category, and the build-vs-buy recommendation that came out of it.

## How the evaluation ran

Three pods, six tools, one shared assessment structure so results could be
compared rather than collected.

Junior practitioners ran the hands-on piloting. That was deliberate. The people
who would use these tools every day are better evaluators than a lead reading
feature matrices, and they hit the friction that demos are designed to hide.

The decision came after the pilots. For each category we asked whether buying
was the right answer at all, then pressure-tested that with internal developers
and designers rather than settling it in a vendor conversation.

<Diagram name="buildbuy" />

The verdicts:

- **Journey mapping: build.** This is a method the practice is known for. Every
  tool we looked at wanted us to adopt its model of what a journey map is,
  which would have meant handing over the thing that differentiates us.
- **Synthetic personas: buy.** Fast-moving and model-dependent. Any advantage we
  built would be gone in two release cycles, and a consultancy shouldn't be
  maintaining that.
- **User research: buy.** Real capability, and commoditized. Building it would
  have been paying to solve a solved problem.

The rule underneath: build only where the capability is the differentiation.
Everywhere else you're paying maintenance, staffing and the opportunity cost
of engineers who could be on client work.

## Outcomes

- **6 tools** piloted across synthetic personas, journey mapping and user
  research
- **2 builds** shortlisted after pressure-testing, avoiding ~**$400K**
- **8 practitioners** developed across 3 pods

## What I'd do differently

I set the build-vs-buy criteria after the pilots rather than before. Running
pilots first meant the criteria were shaped by what we had just seen, which is
a quiet way to rationalize a preference you already had. Writing down what
would make a build worth it in advance would have made the recommendation
harder to argue with, including for me.
