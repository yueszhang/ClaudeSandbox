I led the market-sensing and pilot pillar of an internal AI initiative at
Deloitte, evaluating tools for our design and research teams and deciding, per
tool, whether to buy the vendor product or build the equivalent ourselves.

Internal work, which made it more honest than most vendor evaluations. Nobody
was being sold to and no procurement deadline was forcing an answer. The output
either got used by our own practitioners or it sat there.

## How it ran

A portfolio of pilots running in parallel across synthetic personas, journey
mapping, user research and content generation. Each had a named owner, a defined
evaluation window, and a go/no-go at the end.

Around 15 stakeholder interviews went into the flagship pilot alone, and I drove
its kickoff and evaluation approach.

Practitioners ran the hands-on piloting rather than a lead reading feature
matrices. The people who would use these tools daily are better evaluators, and
they hit the friction demos are built to hide.

I also coordinated with adjacent practices so findings got shared instead of
duplicated. That sounds like housekeeping and was most of the value: three teams
independently piloting the same persona tool is how a firm wastes a quarter.

The verdicts:

- **Journey mapping: build.** This is a method the practice is known for. Every
  tool we looked at wanted us to adopt its model of what a journey map is,
  which would have meant handing over the thing that differentiates us.
- **Synthetic personas: buy.** Fast-moving and model-dependent. Any advantage we
  built would be gone in two release cycles, and a consultancy shouldn't be
  maintaining that.
- **User research: buy.** Real capability, and commoditized. Building it would
  have been paying to solve a solved problem.

The rule underneath: build only where the capability is the differentiation.
Everywhere else you're paying maintenance, staffing and the opportunity cost
of engineers who could be on client work.

## What came out of it

- Parallel pilots across **4 capability areas**, each with an owner, a window
  and a go/no-go
- **~15 stakeholder interviews** on the flagship pilot
- A build-or-buy call on each, pressure-tested with internal developers and
  designers
- One pilot reached a measured efficiency figure across a defined set of test
  conversations

## What I'd do differently

I set the build-vs-buy criteria after the pilots rather than before. Running
pilots first meant the criteria were shaped by what we had just seen, which is
a quiet way to rationalize a preference you already had. Writing down what
would make a build worth it in advance would have made the recommendation
harder to argue with, including for me.
