Internal work, and unusually honest because of it. When the buyer is your own
practice, nobody is being sold to — the evaluation either produces something
people use or it produces a shelf document.

## The situation

Deloitte's Customer Strategy & Design practice needed a view on AI tooling
across three areas of its craft: synthetic personas, journey mapping, and user
research. A crowded, fast-moving vendor landscape, and a practice with real
opinions about its own methods.

## What I owned

The evaluation program — leading eight junior practitioners across three pods,
each pod owning a category, and the build-vs-buy recommendation that came out
of it.

## How the evaluation ran

Three pods, six tools, one shared assessment structure so results could be
compared rather than just collected. Junior practitioners ran the hands-on
piloting, which was deliberate: the people who would use these tools daily are
better evaluators than a lead reading feature matrices, and they surface the
friction that demos hide.

The part that mattered was what happened after the pilots. For each category we
asked whether buying was actually the right answer — and then pressure-tested
that with internal developers and designers rather than deciding on the
strength of a vendor conversation.

The verdicts split cleanly, and not along the lines the vendor demos would have
suggested:

- **Journey mapping — build.** This is a method the practice is known for. The
  mapping approach *is* the differentiation, and the available tools all wanted
  us to adopt their model of what a journey is.
- **Synthetic personas — buy.** Fast-moving, model-dependent, and not something
  a consultancy should be maintaining. Any advantage here would be gone in two
  release cycles.
- **User research — buy.** Real capability, genuinely commoditized. Building it
  would have been paying to reinvent a solved problem.

Two builds survived the pressure test, shortlisted on roughly **$400K** of
avoided spend.

The rule underneath: build only where the capability *is* the differentiation.
Buying a generic version of the thing your practice is known for is how you pay
a vendor to erode your own advantage. Everywhere else, buying wins, and
building is a tax you keep paying — maintenance, staffing, and the opportunity
cost of engineers who could be doing client work.

## Outcomes

- **6 tools** piloted across synthetic personas, journey mapping, and user
  research
- **2 builds** shortlisted after pressure-testing, on ~**$400K** avoided spend
- **8 practitioners** developed across 3 pods

## What I'd do differently

I would set the build-vs-buy criteria before the pilots rather than after.
Running pilots first meant the criteria were partly shaped by what we had just
seen, which is a subtle way to rationalize a preference. Writing down what
would make a build worth it — in advance — would have made the recommendation
harder to argue with.
