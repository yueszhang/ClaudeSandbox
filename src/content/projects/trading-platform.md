I was product owner and PMO lead on a trading system implementation for a global
financial services firm. Two jobs that pull in opposite directions, which is
most of what made it interesting.

As product owner I worked with the product and development teams to solution and
configure **60+ product gaps** before we got anywhere near UAT. As PMO lead I
ran the tracking architecture for **16 workstreams** across three implementation
phases.

## The gaps

Sixty-plus gaps between what the platform did and what the firm needed, found
before user acceptance testing rather than during it.

That timing is the whole point. A gap found in UAT is a defect with an audience:
it has a ticket, a severity, a business stakeholder watching, and a fix that now
competes with the go-live date. The same gap found during configuration is just
a design decision. Same problem, an order of magnitude cheaper.

## Running sixteen workstreams

The PMO side was tracking: **300+ workflows** and **200+ test scripts** across
three phases, plus **20+ critical cross-phase dependencies**.

Cross-phase dependencies are where implementations of this size tend to fail.
Each phase is planned as though it owns its own scope, and the things that span
phases belong to nobody until they slip. Naming twenty of them explicitly and
giving each an owner is unglamorous work that prevented a specific category of
late surprise.

I built **50+ dashboards** and standardized how tracking worked across every
workstream. That standardization cut roughly **5 hours per workstream per week**
of reporting effort, which across 16 workstreams is most of a full-time person
recovered every week.

I also walked **100+ practitioners** through the change management process.

## What I'd do differently

I optimized the reporting before I understood which decisions the reports were
supposed to support. Fifty dashboards is a lot of dashboards. A smaller number
built after watching what people asked for in the weekly would have taken less
time and been used more.
