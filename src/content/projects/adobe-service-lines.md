I spent the summer between MBA years at Adobe Professional Services, working
out which services Adobe should be selling and was not. I catalogued and priced
new service lines with executive stakeholders, then scoped two custom
implementations worth $200K that pulled Adobe Experience Cloud into enterprise
systems.

Ten weeks. Most of the value to me was the change of seat: I had spent four
years scoping and negotiating enterprise software as the buyer, and this was
the same work from the other side.

## The problem

Adobe wanted a bigger implementation pipeline and the open question was which
service lines to add.

New service lines get proposed constantly and most of them die, because they
get argued from anecdote. A salesperson loses a deal, decides there is a gap,
and nothing ever tests that.

## Pricing off evidence

Grounding the catalogue in customer data changed how the executive
conversations went. The claim stopped being that customers seemed to want
something and became a pattern of demand you could point at.

Scoping two of them into real implementations was the test. A service line you
cannot turn into a priced, deliverable engagement is a slide, and scoping is
where the optimistic ones come apart.

## What the buyer-side years contributed

I knew where enterprise buyers get nervous, because I had been the nervous one.
Integration surface area. Dependence on the vendor's own professional services.
Total cost once you count the customization nobody scoped up front.

Those anxieties are predictable. Price a service line without accounting for
them and it looks competitive right up until it does not close.

## Outcomes

- New service lines catalogued and priced with executive stakeholders
- **2 custom implementations** scoped and designed, worth **$200K**
- Adobe Experience Cloud integrated into enterprise systems

## What I'd do differently

I worked mostly from data about customers rather than talking to them. The
analysis held up. Ten weeks is still enough time for a handful of real
conversations, and those would have tested the pricing assumptions in a way
historical data cannot.
